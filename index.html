How this works

Every image is computed from data and physics. No photograph or generative model is used. The renderer works out where each piece of sky is for a given place and time, decides how much light arrives from it and in what colour, then develops the result the way a camera and raw converter would.

Rendering pipeline

  1. Camera ray. Each pixel becomes a direction through a rectilinear lens (or a cylindrical panorama) of the chosen full-frame focal length, heading and tilt.
  2. Sky pass. Galaxy glow, nebulae and atmosphere are evaluated for that direction in floating-point light units. The pass also records how much dust lies along the line of sight, so the star pass can dim stars behind it.
  3. Star pass. Real catalogue stars, fainter synthetic stars, star clusters and planets are drawn as energy-conserving point-spread functions: a Gaussian core plus a faint wide halo, sized in arcminutes.
  4. Composite. Sea, shore, headland and sea stacks reflect and scatter a low-resolution copy of the sky. The camera stage then applies exposure, white balance, vignetting, sensor noise and a filmic tone curve.
  5. Tiling. Large renders are split into 1024 px tiles with overlapping margins, so 8192 px images fit in GPU memory. Every random element comes from a seed, so a settings file always reproduces the same image.

Where the sky is

Latitude, date, year and local solar time give the local sidereal time (LST ≈ 6.656 h + 0.0657 h × day + 1.00274 × solar time). That fixes the rotation between the horizon frame and the equatorial frame. The IAU J2000 galactic rotation matrix then links equatorial and galactic coordinates. With Aim at the core switched on, the page solves the hour angle at which the galactic centre (RA 266.4°, Dec −28.9°) reaches the altitude you ask for. From that it sets the solar time and heading, and warns you if that time falls in daylight.

The Milky Way: real-sky model

With Real-sky landmarks at 100%, the band is assembled from measured data, with procedural detail only below the data's resolution:

  • Overall shape and brightness. Five nested isophotes, contours of equal surface brightness traced by Jose R. Vieira, are filled on the sphere at start-up with an even–odd rule. Each level is blurred by its own amount, so faint outer contours are soft and bright inner ones tighter, and the stack becomes a brightness ramp.
  • Central bulge and inner disk. A slightly boxy bulge and an exponential disk supply the light around the galactic centre, where hand-traced contours are least reliable. Their coordinates are warped with noise so no contour runs as a straight line along the plane.
  • Dust. The Schlegel, Finkbeiner & Davis (1998) far-infrared map gives the dust column E(B−V) across the sky at about 6′. It is used two ways: a smooth column that carves the Great Rift, and a fine-detail layer, stretched slightly along the plane, that draws real lanes, streamers and windows such as the Pipe Nebula and the Rho Ophiuchi clouds. Where the map saturates in the mid-plane, the column is read at a warped position so the rift's edges come out lobed and fingered. On top of this, 1,882 Lynds and Barnard dark nebulae are splatted as soft ellipses at their catalogued sizes and opacity classes.
  • How light passes the dust. Optical depth uses a reddening law (red light gets through more easily than blue), so partly obscured clouds at lane edges turn brown and orange. A small fraction of the light is treated as lying in front of the dust, so lanes never go fully black. The densest spine of the rift is left dark; its flanks carry faint, patchy foreground glow.
  • Star-cloud windows. Low-dust windows such as the Sagittarius and Scutum clouds, M24 and the Lagoon-to-M24 stream add extra light. Noise breaks them into knots, and only local dust dims them.
  • Nebulae. 1,140 HII regions and 117 reflection nebulae from the catalogue add pink and blue glow. Named bright nebulae (Lagoon, Trifid, Omega, Eagle, Rho Ophiuchi, Antares, Carina, Orion and others) are placed at their true positions, sizes and orientations. A faint diffuse hydrogen-alpha glow follows the plane and gathers near dust.
  • Texture. Below about 6′, star-cloud mottling, dust clumps and core filaments come from seeded fractal noise. Its octaves stop at the pixel scale, so a larger render shows finer detail instead of aliasing.

At 0%, the band comes instead from the original 3D model: a ray marched from the Sun's position through an exponential disk, two-armed spiral, barred bulge and fractal dust. That makes a plausible galaxy that is not ours.

Stars, clusters and planets

  • Real stars. All 41,411 stars to magnitude 8 sit at their catalogue positions. Each star's colour comes from its B−V index through Ballesteros' formula for temperature and a blackbody curve. Their magnitudes are apparent, so they are not dimmed by dust a second time.
  • Fainter stars. Fainter stars, down to the Faintest star limit (up to magnitude 12), follow galactic star-count laws. They are about 2.75 times more numerous per magnitude, concentrate toward the plane and bulge, and fade behind dust according to the sky pass. Very faint, unresolved stars become a grain in the glow, set by Star-cloud grain.
  • Clusters. Open and globular clusters to V 9.5 are drawn as member stars plus unresolved glow. The light of their catalogue stars is subtracted first, so the totals match.
  • Planets. Mercury to Saturn come from JPL's approximate Keplerian elements (valid 1800–2050), converted to geocentric J2000 positions. Magnitudes follow the standard phase-angle formulas. Solar time stands in for UT, which shifts a planet by well under a degree.
  • Bright cores. The brightest points get a tighter core, and bright cores clip toward white as they would on a sensor.

Atmosphere

Extinction follows Kasten & Young's air-mass formula with stronger loss at blue wavelengths, so stars and glow redden and fade toward the horizon. Airglow is a thin emission shell that brightens toward the horizon by the van Rhijn factor, coloured between the 557.7 nm green and 630 nm red oxygen lines. Light pollution adds a horizon dome at a chosen bearing and colour. Sky glow adds a broad scattered component that rises toward the horizon.

Sea, shore and land

The sea is a sum of directional wave trains. Each one is band-limited to the pixel footprint and averaged by the long-exposure setting, so a slow shutter smooths the surface while larger swell survives. Reflection uses Schlick's Fresnel term and a rough lookup into a mip-mapped sky panorama, which turns the Milky Way into a soft glitter path. Foam streaks, the wavy wash line and wet pebbles (a Voronoi cell field) are procedural. The headland, its trees, distant hills and sea stacks are silhouettes in angle space, generated from the coastline seed.

Camera and development

Exposure in EV, a sky black point (the background subtraction astrophotographers apply), white balance from blackbody gains with a green–magenta tint, saturation, contrast around mid-grey, cos⁴ vignetting, and shot-plus-colour noise scaled by signal. The tone curve is ACES-style, followed by sRGB encoding and dithering.

How the "Salt flat core" preset was matched

This preset reproduces a reference photograph. Its camera was found by plate-solving: the photo's bright stars were matched against the catalogue. That gave a 21.5 mm lens at latitude 36° with the galactic centre 17.8° up. Jupiter and Saturn then fixed the date as 3 September 2019, to within a day. The galaxy settings were tuned by measuring brightness profiles across the band, the darkness of the rift against the core, and the colour of cloud edges at matched brightness, with an independent critic reviewing each round.

Data sources

  • Milky Way outlines: Jose R. Vieira, as packaged in d3-celestial by Olaf Frohn (BSD-3-Clause).
  • Stars to magnitude 8: Hipparcos and Tycho catalogues (ESA), via d3-celestial.
  • Dust: Schlegel, Finkbeiner & Davis 1998, ApJ 500, 525, "Maps of Dust IR Emission", via sfddata.
  • Dark nebulae, HII regions, reflection nebulae and clusters: Lynds (LDN), Barnard, Sharpless, NGC/IC and Messier entries from the Stellarium DSO catalogue.
  • Planet orbits: E. M. Standish, "Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets", JPL Solar System Dynamics.
  • Methods: Kasten & Young 1989 (air mass); Ballesteros 2012 (B−V to temperature); IAU J2000 galactic frame.

Known limits: the dust map cannot separate near and far dust inside the plane, so the densest part of the rift is shaped partly procedurally. There is no Moon yet, the planets cover Mercury to Saturn only, and real stars stop at magnitude 8; fainter stars are statistical.
